Detroit techno is a genre of electronic music that originated in Detroit, Michigan. It resulted from the melding of African American styles such as funk, electro, and house with synth-pop by artists like Kraftwerk, Giorgio Moroder, and Yellow Magic Orchestra.

origins and influences

Detroit techno was influenced by the influence of futuristic and science-fiction themes relevant to contemporary American society, including Alvin Toffler's book The Third Wave. Musical influences included European synthesizer music, which Derrick May identified as 'classy and clean' and compared to outer space. Juan Atkins acknowledged early enthusiasm for Giorgio Moroder and Kraftwerk, while Derrick May also cited Yellow Magic Orchestra and Ultravox as influences. Kevin Saunderson noted he was more inspired by the idea of making music with electronic equipment. These artists used science fiction imagery to articulate visions of a transformed society.

first wave artists

The first wave of Detroit techno is viewed as consisting of musicians such as Eddie Fowlkes, Blake Baxter, James Pennington, and Juan Atkins, Derrick May, and Kevin Saunderson, who are collectively known as The Belleville Three. Belleville Three members Juan Atkins and Derrick May were influenced by the professores own album E=MC2 by Giorgio Moroder.

development of the local scene

Budding musicians, aspiring DJs, and 'mix' tape traders like Atkins, Saunderson, May, and Fowlkes shared common interests. They found inspiration via the Midnight Funk Association, a late-night radio program hosted by DJ Charles "The Electrifying Mojo" Johnson from 1977 through the mid-1980s. This show featured electronic music by artists such as Tangerine Dream, Kraftwerk, Yellow Magic Orchestra and Giorgio Moroder, alongside new wave music by bands like the B-52's and Devo, and funk sounds of acts like Parliament Funkadelic. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, the local dance music scene was nurtured in high school clubs such as Snobs, Gables, Rafael, Ciabattino, Charivari and Brats. The form was also developed in vacant warehouses, offices, YMCA auditoriums, and local church activity centers.
