The Lydian Chromatic Concept of Tonal Organization is a 1953 jazz music theory book written by George Russell. It serves as the founding text for the Lydian Chromatic Theory (LCT) or Lydian Chromatic Concept (LCC). The work postulates that all music is based on the tonal gravity of the Lydian mode.

theoretical foundations

George Russell believed that dominant function is the driving force behind all harmonic motion. He focused on the Lydian mode because it can be built with fifths; for example, a C Lydian scale is constructed by listing the first seven tones on the circle of fifths starting with C. When these tones are voiced in thirds, they form the preferred form of a major 13 (#11) chord. Russell's theory posits that tonal gravity emanates from the first seven tones of the Lydian mode and shifts as a player ventures further up the circle of fifths.

the lydian chromatic scale

Russell developed a prototype chromatic scale starting on the Lydian Tonic by stacking fifths, skipping the interval between the seventh and eighth tones, and placing the skipped tone at the end. Using C as the Lydian Tonic, this yields a 12-note scale (C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯, G♯, E♭/D♯, B♭/A♯, F/E♯, D♭/C♯) containing only Pythagorean intervals.

influence on jazz

The Lydian Chromatic Concept has had a far-reaching effect, particularly in modal jazz. It was a crucial influence on Miles Davis and John Coltrane, who used the theory to record the album Kind of Blue. Many conservatories teach the theory to varying degrees. Art Farmer described it as opening the door to countless means of melodic expression, while critic Joachim-Ernst Berendt described it as the first work to derive a theory of jazz harmony from the immanent laws of jazz.

application in composition

John Coltrane's modal jazz is typically analyzed using Russell's method. In his piece "Giant Steps," Coltrane travels through a succession of three parent Lydian Chromatic scales: E Lydian, A♭ Lydian, and C Lydian.
