Britpop was a British music and cultural movement of the 1990s that produced bright, catchy alternative rock emphasizing national identity and British culture. The movement emerged as a reaction against American grunge and British shoegaze, bringing British alternative rock into the mainstream.

overview

Britpop was often described as a cultural moment rather than a strictly defined musical genre, with leading bands sharing influences from 1960s British pop, 1970s glam and punk rock, and 1980s indie pop. The movement's peak years were between 1995 and 1996, and it was associated with the broader Cool Britannia phenomenon. The acts most frequently labeled as Britpop's "big four" were Oasis, Blur, Suede, and Pulp. The imagery of the era was characterized by Union Jack symbols, working-class identity, and 'lad culture'.

the big four: suede and pulp

Suede, formed in London in 1989, drew from glam rock and post-punk. Their debut album Suede reached number one on the UK Albums Chart and was the fastest-selling debut album in nearly a decade, winning the Mercury Music Prize. While they distanced themselves from the Britpop label, they were part of the 'big four' by 1994. Pulp, formed in Sheffield in 1978, became reluctant figureheads of the movement due to their "kitchen sink drama" lyrics and references to British culture. Their album Different Class (1995) won the Mercury Prize and reached number one on the UK Albums Chart.

origins and influences

Britpop began when Blur's "Popscene" and Suede's "The Drowners" were released in the spring of 1992. The movement drew heavily from the traditions of 1960s guitar-based British pop, with influences including the Kinks, early Pink Floyd, and the Beatles. Other precursors include the Smiths, the Stone Roses, and Happy Mondays from Manchester's indie scene, as well as the La's hit single "There She Goes".
