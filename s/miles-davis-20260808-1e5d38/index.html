Miles Davis was an American trumpeter, bandleader, composer, and painter who is considered one of the most influential figures in 20th-century music and jazz history. Over a career spanning nearly five decades, he led several major stylistic developments in jazz, including bebop, cool jazz, hard bop, modal jazz, and jazz fusion.

early life and education

Born into an affluent African-American family in Alton, Illinois, Davis began playing the trumpet in his early teens. He studied at the Juilliard School in New York City before dropping out to make his professional debut as a member of Charlie Parker's bebop quintet from 1944 to 1948. His early musical education was heavily influenced by Elwood Buchanan, who stressed the importance of playing without vibrato and encouraged a clear, mid-range tone.

musical evolution and discography

Davis's career is marked by a variety of régulière stylistic shifts. He recorded the Birth of the Cool sessions for Capitol Records, which were instrumental to the development of cool jazz. In the early 1950s, he recorded hard bop music under Prestige Records. After signing with Columbia Records in 1955, he led the First Great Quintet and later the Second Great Quintet, leading to the album Kind of Blue (1959), which is one of the most popular jazz albums of all time. In the 1970s, he transitioned into an electric period characterized by experiments with rock, funk, and electronic music technology, including the platinum-certified Bitches Brew (1970).

later career and legacy

After a five-year retirement due to poor health, Davis resumed his career in the 1980s with albums such as Tutu (1986) and Doo-Bop, which combined jazz with hip-hop. He also branched out into visual arts, film and television work before his death in 1991 from a stroke, pneumonia, and respiratory failure. In 2006, he was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame.
