Miles Davis was an American trumpeter, bandleader, composer, and painter who is considered one of the most influential figures in jazz and 20th-century music. Over a career spanning nearly five decades, he led several major stylistic developments in jazz, including bebop, cool jazz, hard bop, modal jazz, and jazz fusion.

early life and education

Born into an affluent African-American family in Alton, Illinois, Davis began playing the trumpet in his early teens. He studied at the Juilliard School in New York City before dropping out to make his professional debut with Charlie Parker's bebop quintet from 1944 to 1948. His early musical education was heavily influenced by Elwood Buchanan, who emphasized a clear, mid-range tone without vibrato.

musical evolution and discography

Davis's career is characterized by a constant evolution of style. After recording the Birth of the Cool sessions for Capitol Records, which were instrumental to the development of cool jazz, he recorded early hard bop music under Prestige Records in the early 1950s. He later signed with Columbia Records, where he released acclaimed albums such as 'Round About Midnight (1955) and Kind of Blue (1959), the latter being one of the most popular jazz albums of all time. In the 1960s, he pioneered post-bop through albums like E.S.P. and Miles Smiles, before transitioning into an electric period in the 1970s that experimented with rock, funk, and electronic music technology. This era produced the platinum-certified Bitches Brew (1970), which helped spark a resurgence in jazz fusion's commercial popularity.

the great quintets

Davis led several influential ensembles, most notably the First Great Quintet (1955-1958) featuring John Coltrane, Red Garland, Paul Chambers, and Philly Joe Jones. This group expanded to a sextet in 1958 with the addition of the alto saxophone player Cannonball Adderley. The Second Great Quintet (1964-1968) consisting of Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter, and Tony Williams, pioneered a more abstract approach often referred to as "time, no changes," incorporating elements of free jazz. He also led the 'Lost Quintet' from 1968 to 1970, which represented his transition into jazz fusion music with electronic instruments.

later career and death

After a five-year retirement due to poor health, Davis resumed his career in the 1980s with albums such as The Man with the Horn (1981) and Tutu (1986), employing pop sounds and younger musicians. He performed sold-out concerts worldwide and branched out into visual arts, film, and television work before dying in 1991 from the combined effects of a stroke, pneumonia and respiratory failure at age 65.
