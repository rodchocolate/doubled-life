Signified and signifier are the two primary components of a sign in semiotics. The signified is the concept or what the sign represents, while the signifier is the observable aspect of the sign itself.

conceptual framework

The idea was first proposed by Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure, who divided the sign into a signifier ('sound-image') and a signified ('concept'). In Saussure's view, these components are purely psychological forms rather than substance. While contemporary commentators often describe the signifier as the material form (something that can be seen, heard, touched, smelled or tasted) and the signified as the conceptual ideal form, the relationship between them is arbitrary, meaning there is no logical connection. A sign is only understood when the relationship between its two components is agreed upon through a conventional relationship.

theoretical developments

Following Saussure, other semioticians such as Roland Barthes used signs to explain denotation (literal meanings) and connotation (cultural meanings). Umberto Eco concluded that signs can be used to lie if they can be used to tell the truth. Jean Baudrillard discussed hyperreality, where the signifier becomes more important than the signified. In depth psychology and philosophy, Jacques Lacan repurposed Freud's ideas to describe a 'barrier' of repression between signifiers and the signified. Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari developed the idea of 'faciality' in their theory of schizoanalysis, referring to the interplay of signifiers in the process of subjectification.

floating signifiers

A floating signifier is a signifier that lacks a clear, single referent and can be interpreted in different ways. Originating from Claude Lévi-Strauss's anthropological research, Daniel Chandler defines it as a signifier with a a vague, unspecifiable or non-existent signified. Examples of floating signifiers include the Rorschach inkblot test, money (described by David A. Westbrook as a 'perpetually floating signifier'), and the term 'AI' according to Lucy Suchman. Ernesto Laclau distinguishes between 'empty' signifiers—which result from a diluted reference to a particular signified due to increased appeal—and 'floating' signifiers, which fluctuate between different forms of articulation in different projects.
