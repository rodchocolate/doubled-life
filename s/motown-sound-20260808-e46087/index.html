The Motown sound is a style of soul music characterized by a mainstream pop-influenced sound and appeal. It was produced by Motown and its main subsidiary labels, such as Gordy and Soul, during the 1960s.

overview

The Motown sound was developed by an African American-owned label that achieved crossover success with white audiences, playing a vital role in the racial integration of popular music. Between 1960 and 1969, 79 Motown songs reached the top ten of the Billboard Hot 100. The style's appeal was further enhanced by the lapped-up pop influence,- a characteristic that crossover success was achieved through this mainstream pop-influenced sound.

musical characteristics and influence

A key component of the 'Motown Sound' was the nimble bass playing of James Jamerson. Jamerson, an American bassist who played on nearly every Motown recording between 1963 and 1968, was uncredited on most hits in the the 1960s and early 1970s. His style departed from the simple repetitive patterns of R&B, rock and roll, and country music of the time, instead relying on chromatic runs, syncopation, ghost notes, and inversions. Jamerson's melodic lines, which were locked to the drum groove, became an indispensable part of the Motown sound by the mid-1960s.
