The term 'site' most often refers to a location on the Earth's surface or elsewhere, as well as archaeological, construction, and campsites. In the digital realm, it is commonly used to describe a website, which is a set of related web pages with a common domain name.

websites

A website is any web page whose content is identified by a common domain name and published on at least one web server. These sites are typically dedicated to a particular topic or purpose, such as commerce, education, entertainment, news, or social media. Navigation is guided by hyperlinking between pages, often starting with a home page. Publicly-accessible websites collectively form the World Wide Web, which was created in 1989 by Tim Berners-Lee. While 'web site' was the original spelling, 'website' has become the standard. The number of websites globally has fluctuated; for example, in 2009 Netcraft reported 215,675,903 sites, and by January 2020, there were 1,295,973,827 websites. An estimated 85 percent of all websites are inactive.

types of websites

Websites are categorized as either static or dynamic. A static website is primarily coded in HTML and CSS, providing consistent, standard information to all visitors over an extended period. Examples include brochure websites or simple marketing examples. Static sites may use server side includes (SSI) for editing convenience. Dynamic websites change or customize themselves automatically and frequently. They are generated 'on the fly' by computer code using software systems like Java Server Pages (JSP), ColdFusion (CFML), and CGI, or frameworks for languages such as PHP, Python, Ruby, and Perl. Dynamic sites can be interactive, using HTML forms, browser cookies, or JavaScript to modify page contents.

site a

Site A was a World War II research facility near Chicago operated by the University of Chicago's Metallurgical Laboratory for the Manhattan Project. It was the site of Chicago Pile-2 (a reconstructed and enlarged version of the world's first nuclear reactor) and the first heavy-water reactor, Chicago Pile-3. Research was conducted under contract to the United States' Office of Scientific Research and Development. After the war, it became the first home of Argonne National Laboratory. The site was returned to the public forest agency in 1956, but continues to be managed by the Department of Energy's Office of Legacy Management as the Site A/Plot M Disposal Site.

الماضى (history) and decommissioning of site a

Site A was acquired in 1942 after Professor Arthur Compton identified a suitable location in the Argonne Forest. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers leased 1,025 acres from the forest preserve district. After the first self-sustaining chain reaction demonstration at the University of Chicago, Enrico Fermi's research group moved to Site A in March 1943, bringing CP-1, which was reconfigured as Chicago Pile-2. In May 1944, the heavy water-moderated reactor CP-3 was operated. The reactors operated until 1954 and were then defueled and demolished. Plot M served as a dump for low-level radioactive waste from 1943 to 1949, including tritium, uranium, and fission products. While buried remnants remain, the site has been certified safe for ordinary public access by the EPA and Illinois since the late 1980s.
