Reggae is a music genre that originated in Jamaica in the late 1960s and also refers to the modern popular music of Jamaica and its diaspora. The genre was effectively named by the 1968 single "Do the Reggay" by Toots and the Maytals.

origins and influences

Reggae developed from earlier Jamaican genres such as rocksteady, ska, and mento. It is rooted in traditional drumming styles including burru, Pukkumina, Nyabinghi, Revival Zion, and Kumina. Additionally, the genre incorporates elements of jazz, rhythm and blues, and traditional African folk rhythms.

musical characteristics

Reggae is distinguished by a slower tempo than rocksteady or ska, with a strong emphasis on the downbeat in the bass and drum. It features short, staccato piano or guitar chords on the offbeat and a central role for the bass guitar, which has a high-frequency reduction to accentuate the low end. Vocals are often delivered in Iyaric, Jamaican English, or Jamaican Patois, with lyrical themes ranging from love, religion, and leisure activities to social and political commentary.

cultural connections and global spread

Reggae is closely associated with the Afrocentric religion of Rastafari, which emerged in Jamaica in the 1930s. From the 1970s onwards, its international success helped spread Rastafarian beliefs. The genre has reached audiences worldwide, including Latin America (reggae en Español), Africa (boosted by Bob Marley's 1980 visit to Zimbabwe), and the United Kingdom, where it has been popular since the late 1960s.

reggae fusion

Reggae fusion is a fusion genre that mixes reggae and/or dancehall with other genres such as rock, pop, soul, jazz, R&B, funk, disco, electronic, Latin music, and hip-hop/rap. While artists have been mixing these styles since the early 1970s, the term was coined in the late 1990s. The subgenre evolved predominantly from dancehall music of the early 1990s and late 1980s. In Europe, a style known as Euro reggae became a trend within Eurodance music.
