Nonsense syllables such as 'bebop' and 'rebop' are vocables used in scat singing. By 1945, their use was widespread in R&B music, exemplified by Lionel Hampton's song 'Hey! Ba-Ba-Re-Bop'.

etymology and origins

The term 'bebop' is derived from nonsense syllables used in scat singing. The first known example of the same was recorded in 1928 in McKinney's Cotton Pickers' 'Four or Five Times'. Other early examples include Jack Teagarden's 1936 recording of 'I'se a Muggin'' and several 1939 recordings featuring the variation 'rebop'. The term was little used until it was applied to the music style associated with it in the mid-1940s. Theories on its origin include that it sounded like something Charlie Christian hummed, or that it was coined by audiences who heard Dizzy Gillespie scat compositions to his players. Another theory suggests it derives from the 'Arriba! Arriba!' cry used by Latin American bandleaders.

application in r&b music

In R&B music, nonsense syllables were used to convey excitement. In 1945, Helen Humes recorded 'Be-Baba-Leba', an R&B hit that featured lyrics such as 'oooh oooh oooh baba-leba'. This was followed by Lionel Hampton's 1946 recording of 'Hey! Ba-Ba-Re-Bop', which replaced salacious verses with nonsense rhymes like "Matilda Brown told old King Tut, 'If you can't say rebop keep your big mouth shut', say hey ba-ba-re-bop...".

later influence on rock and roll

The use of nonsense syllables to convey excitement was passed on to later songs in 1956, such as Little Richard's 'Tutti Frutti' with the phrase 'awopbopaloobop alopbamboom' and Gene Vincent's 'Be-Bop-A-Lula'.
