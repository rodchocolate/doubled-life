William John Evans (1929–1980) was an American jazz pianist and composer known for his influence on jazz pianists today through his use of impressionist harmony, block chords, innovative chord voicings, and rhythmically independent "singing" melodic lines. He worked primarily as the leader of his own trio.

early life and education

Born in Plainfield, New Jersey, Evans grew up with a brother, Harry, to whom he was very close. He began taking formal piano lessons at age six with Helen Leland, who avoided a heavy technical approach. Encouraged by parents, he also studied violin, flute, and piccolo. During high school, Evans was exposed to jazz via radio broadcasts of Tommy Dorsey's and Harry James's bands, and was influenced by harmony theory from bassist George Platt. He later attended Southeastern Louisiana College on a flute scholarship, where he graduated with degrees in piano and music education. He also studied at the Mannes School of Music in New York City, where he majored in composition and received an artist diploma.

professional career

In 1955, Evans moved to New York City and worked with theorist George Russell. In 1958, he joined Miles Davis's sextet, recording the best-selling jazz album of all time, Kind of Blue, on which he played piano. In late 1959, Evans left Davis's band and formed a seminal modern jazz trio with bassist Scott LaFaro and drummer Paul Motian. This group recorded studio albums such as Portrait in Jazz and Explorations, and live recordings at the Village Vanguard jazz club, including Sunday at the Village Vanguard and Waltz for Debby. Following the death of Scott LaFaro in a car crash, Evans eventually reemerged with a new trio featuring Chuck Israels on bass. He later worked with bassist Eddie Gómez for 11 years starting in 1966.

titles

Evans's compositions, including "Waltz for Debby" and "Time Remembered", have become jazz standards. In 1963, he recorded the Grammy Award–winning solo album Conversations with Myself using overdubbing technology. In the mid-1970s, he collaborated with singer Tony Bennett on two critically acclaimed albums: The Tony Bennett/Bill Evans Album (1975) and Together Again (1977). Throughout his career, Evans received 31 Grammy nominations and seven awards, and was inducted into the DownBeat Jazz Hall of Fame.
