Disco is a genre of dance music and a subculture that emerged from the urban nightlife scene of the United States in the late 1960s. Its sound is characterized by four-on-the-floor beats, syncopated basslines, and various instruments including synthesizers, electric pianos, and brass. The term 'disco' is shorthand for discotheque, a French word meaning 'library of phonograph records'.

origins and development

Disco music began as a mixture of music from venues popular among Italian Americans, Latino Americans, and African Americans in Philadelphia, Baltimore, and New York City (specifically Brooklyn) during the late 1960s to mid-to-late 1970s. It was developed further by artists and record producers from Europe and the United Kingdom, and later became a reaction by the 1960s counterculture against the dominance of rock music and the stigmatization of dance music. The genre's popularity in the United States grew with the help of hit films such as Thank God It's Friday (1978) and Saturday Night Fever (1977), which helped popularize the genre mainstreamly. In New York, WKTU became the most popular station in the country after switching to disco in 1978.

musical characteristics

Disco typically features soaring, often-reverberated vocals over a background 'pad' of electric pianos and 'chicken-scratch' rhythm guitars. The rhythm is driven by a steady four-on-the-floor beat set by a bass drum, a heavy syncopated bass line, and hi-hat patterns with an open hissing hi-hat on the off-beat. In Philly dance and Salsoul disco, the sound was enriched with orchestral instruments such as flute, violin, viola, cello, trumpet, saxophone, trombone, flugelhorn, French horn, English horn, oboe and timpani. Other backing keyboard instruments include string synthesizers, electric organ, piano, and electromechanical keyboards like the Hohner Clavinet, Wurlitzer electric piano, and Fender Rhodes electric piano. Donna Summer's 1977 song 'I Feel Love', produced by Giorgio Moroder, was one of the first disco tracks to use the synthesizer.

subculture and decline

The disco subculture included nightclubs known as discotheques, which were a French invention imported to the US by Olivier Coquelin with the opening of Le Club in Manhattan on New Year's Eve 1960. Popular venues like Studio 54 in Manhattan were frequented by celebrities and attendees often wore extravagant, expensive outfits consisting of loose flowing pants or dresses. The scene was also associated with promiscuity and a drug subculture involving cocaine and quaaludes (nicknamed 'disco biscuits'). Disco declined as a major trend in the US following the July 12, 1979, Disco Demolition Night at Comiskey Park in Chicago, which ended in a riot. While it sharply declined in popularity in the US during the early 1980s, it remained popular in Italy and some European countries throughout the 1980s, and later became trendy in India and the Middle East where it was blended with regional folk styles like belly dancing and ghazals.

legacy and revivals

Disco would eventually become a key influence in the development of house music, electronic dance music, hip-hop, new wave, dance-punk, and post-disco. The style has had several revivals since the 1990s, and a revival that began in the early 2010s gained great popularity in the early 2020s.

facts

  • Mainstream Popularity Drivers: Films such as Saturday Night Fever (1977) and Thank God It's Friday (1978).
  • Key Instruments: Four-on-the-floor beats, syncopated basslines, string sections, brass and horns, electric pianos, synthesizers, and electric rhythm guitars.
  • label own: The 'rooster scratch' sound is achieved by lightly pressing guitar strings against the fretboard and then quickly releasing them to get a slightly muted poker sound while strumming close to the bridge.
