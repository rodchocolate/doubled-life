Southern soul, also known as country soul, is a type of soul and country music that emerged from the Southern United States. It originated from a combination of styles including early R&B, country, blues (both jump and 12 bar), and gospel influence from Southern black churches.

Southern soul, also known as country soul, is a type of soul and country music that emerged from the Southern United States. It originated from a combination of styles including early R&B, country, blues (both jump and 12 bar), and gospel influence from Southern black churches.

musical characteristics

Southern soul is described as a driving, energetic variety of music that combines R&B with southern gospel music influences. Important elements to the soul groove include drums, bass guitar, horn section, organ, and gospel roots vocal. This rhythmic force served as a strong influence in the rise of funk music.

history and development

The genre reached its peak during the 1960s, particularly through the popularity of Memphis soul and the Muscle Shoals sound. Key record labels such as Stax Records and Atlantic Records were significant contributors; Atlantic distributed Percy Sledge's "When a Man Loves a Woman," recorded at Rick Hall's FAME Studios in Muscle Shoals, Alabama. Other labels included Jewel Records, founded by Stan Lewis in 1963 in Shreveport, Louisiana, and Memphis-based Hi Records and Goldwax Records. Malaco Records also introduced artists such as Bobby Bland and Johnnie Taylor.

notable artists

Prominent musicians associated with the genre include Georgia natives Otis Redding and James Brown, as well as Al Green, Bobby "Blue" Bland, Rufus Thomas, and Aretha Franklin. Otis Redding was influenced by Sam Cooke and Little Richard. In the later years, artists such as Sharon Jones, Charles Bradley, and Bobby Rush continued to record and perform Southern soul music after 1990.
