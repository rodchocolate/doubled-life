Jack DeJohnette (1942–2025) was an American jazz drummer, pianist and composer who recorded more than 35 albums as a band leader. He was inducted into the Modern Drummer Hall of Fame in 2007, won two Grammy Awards, and received six other nominations.

early life and musical beginnings

Born in Chicago, Illinois, DeJohnette was raised by his grandmother Rosalie Ann Wood. He possessed perfect pitch and began studying piano at age four with Antoinette Rich. At 13, he switched to drums, though he piano remained his first instrument. He was taught drumming techniques by Bobby Miller Jr and was inspired by his uncle, Roy Wood Sr., a Chicago jazz disc jockey. In Chicago, DeJohnette played R&B, hard bop, and avant-garde music, leading his own groups and playing with Richard Abrams and Roscoe Mitchell. He also occasionally played drums for the Sun Ra Arkestra. Before moving to New York City in 1966, he was well-known in Chicago's jazz clubs; notably, he once sat in for Elvin Jones during a residency by John Coltrane's quintet.

career development and collaborations

After moving to New York City in 1966, DeJohnette joined the Charles Lloyd Quartet, where he first met Keith Jarrett. He later worked with musicians such as Jackie McLean, Abbey Lincoln, Betty Carter, and Bill Evans' trio in 1968. In 1969, he replaced Tony Williams in Miles Davis's live band, recognized by Davis for his ability to combine rock and roll grooves with jazz improvisation. DeJohnette was the primary drummer on the album Bitches Brew, which he and other musicians viewed as innovative yet unstructured and fragmentary. He remained with Davis until mid-1971, contributing to albums such as Live-Evil, Jack Johnson, and On the Corner.
