Fela Kuti was a Nigerian musician and political activist who is regarded as the principal innovator of Afrobeat. This music genre combines West African music with American funk and jazz. He was described by AllMusic as a "musical and sociopolitical voice" of international significance.

early life and education

Fela Kuti was born Olufela Olusegun Oludotun Ransome-Kuti on 15 October 1938 in Abeokuta, Nigeria, into an upper-middle-class family. His mother, Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti, was an anti-colonial feminist and his father, Israel Oludotun Ransome-Kuti, was an Anglican minister and school principal. Kuti attended Abeokuta Grammar School before being invited to London in 1958 by his brother Beko to study music at the Trinity College of Music, where he preferred the trumpet. In London, he formed the band Koola Lobitos, which played a fusion of jazz and highlife.

musical evolution and afrobeat

Kuti developed a style he called Afrobeat, which combined traditional Yoruba music, Apala, funk, jazz, highlife, salsa, and calypso. After returning to Nigeria in 1963, he re-formed Koola Lobitos and trained as a radio producer for the Nigerian Broadcasting Corporation. He also played with Victor Olaiya and his All-Stars. Later, in 1969, he took his band, then renamed Nigeria 70, Nigeria 70 to the United States. A ten-month stay in Los Angeles where he discovered the Black Power movement through Sandra Smith influenced his political views and music. Following his return to return to Nigeria, the group was renamed Africa '70. As lyrical themes shifted from love to social issues, his music became popular among Africans but unpopular with the government.

political activism and conflict

In 1970, Kuti founded the Kalakuta Republic commune, which he declared independent from the Nigerian state. He also established a nightclub called the Afrika Shrine (originally the Afro-Spot), where he performed regularly. He changed his name to Anikulapo, meaning "He who carries death in his pouch," and dropped the surname "Ransome" because he considered it a slave name. To reach a wider African audience, he decided to sing in Pidgin English. In 1977, Kuti released the album Zombie, which criticized Nigerian soldiers using a zombie metaphor. This led to a military raid on the Kalakuta Republic in 1977 where 1,000 soldiers burned the commune and destroyed his studio and master tapes. During the raid, Kuti was severely beaten and his mother, Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti, was fatally injured. He responded by delivering his mother's coffin to the residence of General Olusegun Obasanjo and writing the songs "Coffin for Head of State" and "Unknown Soldier."

later years and legacy

// a bit more on later years: he was jailed by the government of Muhammadu Buhari in 1984, but released after 20 months. He continued to record and perform through the 1980s and 1990s. Since his death in 1997, reissues and compilations of his music have been overseen by his son, Femi Kuti. In 2026, he was posthumously inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in the early/musical influence category.
