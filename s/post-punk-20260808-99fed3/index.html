Post-punk is a loosely defined music genre and period that emerged in the late 1970s following punk rock. It is characterized by an experimental approach that incorporates avant-garde sensibilities and non-rock influences, moving beyond raw simplicity and rock clichés.

origins and etymology

The term 'post-punk' was first known to be used in a 1976 interview with painter and poet Jack Micheline in New York poetry magazine Contact. In November 1977, journalist Jon Savage wrote an editorial for Sounds magazine titled 'New Musick', where he described the scene as 'post punk projections'. He noted that bands like Siouxsie and the Banshees, Wire, and The Slits were exploring 'harsh urban scrapings' and 'massively accented drumming'. Some journalists used the term 'art punk' to describe artists who combined garage rock's adrenaline rush with intelligence. In March 1979, Simon Frith proposed the term 'Afterpunk', though this was considered a non-starter.

musical characteristics and influences

Post-punk musicians departed from punk's raw simplicity to adopt a broader, more experimental approach. They drew influence from German krautrock, funk, electronic music, jazz, dance music, and the production techniques of dub and disco. Additionally, they were influenced by ideas from modernist art, cinema, literature, and politics. Beyond music, post-punk artists established independent record labels, produced fanzines, and created visual art and multimedia performances.

regional scenes and legacy

Regional scenes developed across Europe, including the Netherlands' Ultra movement, Germany's Neue Deutsche Welle, Spain's La Movida Madrileña, and coldwave scenes in France, Poland, and Belgium. The original era provided a foundation for British new pop and the Second British Invasion in the United States. It also influenced various alternative genres such as gothic rock, dark wave, dream pop, shoegaze, and neo-psychedelia. In the 2000s, New York bands incorporated these influences into indie rock, leading to a post-punk revival characterized by a 'back-to-basics' guitar rock sound and an emphasis on 'rock authenticity'.
