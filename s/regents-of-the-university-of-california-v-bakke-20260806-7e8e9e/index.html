Regents of the University of California v. Bakke is a 1978 landmark Supreme Court decision regarding whether preferential treatment for minorities could reduce educational opportunities for whites without violating the Constitution. The court upheld affirmative action in general, allowing race to be one of several factors in college admissions policy, but ruled that specific racial quotas were impermissible.

case background

Allan P. Bakke, a former Marine officer and engineer, was rejected for admission to the University of California, Davis, School of Medicine. He challenged the constitutionality of the school's affirmative-action program, which set aside 16 out of 100 seats for minority students. The California Supreme Court struck down the program as violative of the rights of white applicants and ordered Bakke admitted. Following this, the U.S. Supreme Court accepted the case.

supreme court ruling

The ruling was highly fractured, with nine justices issuing six opinions. Justice Lewis F. Powell Jr. wrote the judgment of the court. Justice Powell found that diversity in the classroom to be a compelling state interest, meaning affirmative action in general was allowed under the Constitution and Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. However, the majority of justices felt the University of California, Davis's program went too far by using specific racial quotas, which were struck down and Bakke was admitted.

legal precedent and evolution

The court's position in Bakke was later addressed in 2003 in Grutter v. Bollinger, where the court upheld Powell's position. This position was eventually reversed in 2023 in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, where the Supreme Court found that affirmative action in student admissions violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.
