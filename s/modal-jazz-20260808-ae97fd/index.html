Modal jazz is a form of jazz that utilizes musical modes rather than relying on a single tonal center across a piece. It was most popular during the 1950s and 1960s, though it continues to exert influence today.

Modal jazz is a form of jazz that utilizes musical modes rather than relying on a single tonal center across a piece. It was most popular during the 1950s and 1960s, though it continues to exert influence today.

harmony and modulation

Modal improvisation emphasizes scales or modes over a sequence of changing chords. While chords are still used, they are generally drawn from the prevailing mode. To establish the mode, modal accompaniment often uses repeated use of the modal tonic in the bass, while chords voiced in fourths can provide changing harmonies above it. Modulation—the change of key or tonal center—can involve movement between modes as well as major and minor keys. For example, in Miles Davis's "So What", the A sections use D Dorian and the bridge uses E-flat Dorian, shifting the tonal center up a semitone while retaining the same mode. In "Milestones", the A sections use G Dorian and the B sections use A Aeolian.

history

In bebop and hard bop, musicians typically used chords to provide the background for solos, with soloists playing improvised themes over repeated chord progressions. By the 1950s, this approach had become so dominant that sidemen were sometimes given only a list of chords. Towards the end of the 1950s, spurred by George Russell's experiments, musicians such as Miles Davis, Freddie Hubbard, Bill Evans, Herbie Hancock, and Wayne Shorter began using a modal approach. Earlier precursors to this sound occurred in Juan Tizol's work due to an 'inversion' technique, and in Sun Ra's 1950 rehearsals with a small group including Harold Ousley, Vernel Fournier, and Wilbur Ware. Additionally, Bud Powell's "Glass Enclosure" (1953) and "Un Poco Loco" (1951) used Lydian chords based on the Lydian mode.

significant compositions and artists

Miles Davis's 1958 composition "Milestones" and his 1959 album Kind of Blue are landmark examples of modal jazz. John Coltrane's quartet from 1960 to 1965, featuring Elvin Jones, McCoy Tyner, and Reggie Workman and Jimmy Garrison, extensively explored the limits of modal improvisation. Coltrane's albums Africa/Brass (1961), Live! at the Village Vanguard (1962), Crescent (1964), A Love Supreme (1964), and Meditations (1965) are recognized as examples of the genre. Other significant compositions include "So What" and "Flamenco Sketches" by Miles Davis, "Impressions" by John Coltrane, "Soul Scene" by Justin Chart, and "Peace Piece" by Bill Evans.

facts

  • Popularity Peak: 1950s and 1960s
  • Dorian Mode Usage: The Dorian mode is the natural minor scale with a raised sixth.
  • Crossover Genre: Spiritual jazz draws from modal jazz, and employs elements such as the Dorian mode and quartal harmonies.
  • Lydian Mode Precursors: Bud Powell's 1951 "Un Poco Loco" and 1953 "Glass Enclosure" used Lydian chords.
  • Crossover Artist: John Coltrane's quartet from 1960 to 1965 was a key figure in modal jazz exploration.
