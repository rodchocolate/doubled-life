Bob Dylan is an American singer-songwriter described as one of the greatest songwriters of all time. Over his 69-year career, he has been a major figure in popular culture, infusing folk music with intellectualism and poetic expressions.

early life and education

Born Robert Allen Zimmerman on May 24, 1941, in Duluth, Minnesota, Dylan's paternal grandparents emigrated from the Russian Empire to the United States. As a teenager, he formed several bands while attending Hibbing High School, including the Golden Chords. After enrolling at the University of Minnesota in 1959, he began performing at the Ten O'Clock Scholar coffeehouse and shifted his focus from rock and roll to American folk music, during which time he adopted the name 'Bob Dylan'.

musical career and evolution

Dylan moved to New York City in 1961 to pursue a music career, influenced by Woody Guthrie and Robert Johnson's blues. He played at clubs around Greenwich Village in the early 1960s. His breakthrough album, The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan (1963), propelled him to international fame and featured anthems for civil rights and antiwar movements such as 'Blowin' in the Wind'. He later created controversy by using electrically amplified rock instrumentation on albums like Bringing It All Back Home and Highway 61 Revisited. Following a motorcycle crash in 1966, he ceased touring for seven years, during which time up to 1975 produced The Basement Tapes with members of The Band.

works and achievements

Dylan has released 40 studio albums and 21 live albums. He is one of the best-selling musicians of all time, with an estimated 125 million records sold worldwide. His accolades include a Pulitzer Prize special citation in 2008, ten Grammy Awards, an Academy Award, and the 2016 Nobel Prize in Literature for creating new poetic expressions within the great American song tradition.
