music in film on screen

Music in film encompasses original compositions, known as scores, alongside pre-existing songs that accompany motion pictures. A film score consists of orchestral, instrumental, or choral pieces called cues, timed to enhance the dramatic narrative and emotional impact of scenes. This music is typically written by composers under the guidance of a film's director or producer.

A film score is original music specifically written to accompany a film or television program. It constitutes a number of orchestral, instrumental, or choral pieces called cues that are timed to begin and end at certain moments during the film. The term is less frequently applied to music for live theatre, radio programs, or video games, which are typically referred to as soundtracks or incidental music. Songs such as pop or rock songs are usually not considered part of a film's score, though they form part of the soundtrack; however, scores occasionally feature lyrics when sung by choirs or soloists as part of a cue.

Film scores encompass an enormous variety of styles depending on the nature of the films they accompany. While the majority are orchestral works rooted in Western classical music, many are influenced by jazz, rock, pop, blues, new-age, ambient music, and a wide range of ethnic and world music styles. Since the 1950s, scores have increasingly included electronic elements, with many modern scores featuring a hybrid of orchestral and electronic instruments. Digital technology has allowed some scores to rely on digital samples to imitate acoustic instruments or be created wholly by composers using composition software and synthesizers.

The composer usually enters the creative process towards the end of filming, watching an unpolished 'rough cut' with the director to identify scenes requiring original music. This process, known as spotting, involves taking precise timing notes for each cue. While scores are generally edited to fit the final film cut, some filmmakers edit their films to match pre-existing music; examples include Edgar Wright's Baby Driver and Steven Spielberg editing the finale of E.T. to John Williams' score. Composers sometimes write music based on scripts or storyboards without seeing the film, granting them more freedom regarding cue lengths.

A primary goal when writing for film is to sync dramatic events on screen with musical events in the score. Composers work using SMPTE timecode and may use sequencing software, mathematic formulas, or free timing techniques involving streamers and punches on the film. When conducting live performers, a written click track may be used to establish constant tempo without the stiffness of a metronome. Generally, a leeway of 3–4 frames late or early is considered sufficiently accurate for syncing music to picture.
