me at the zoo

A zoo, short for zoological garden, is a facility where animals are kept in enclosures for exhibition and often bred for conservation purposes. The term is derived from the Ancient Greek 'zōion' meaning animal and '-logia' meaning study of. In the United States, zoos are visited by over 181 million people annually.

The abbreviation 'zoo' first appeared in print in the United Kingdom around 1847 for the Clifton Zoo, though it was not until approximately 20 years later that the the term became popular through Alfred Vance's rhyming song 'Walking in the Zoo'. The London Zoological Gardens, opened for scientific study in 1828 and to the public in 1847, was initially known as the 'Gardens and Menagerie of the Zoological Society of London'. Other terms such as 'zoological park' were used for facilities in Washington, D.C., Halifax, Nova Scotia, and the Bronx in New York. In the late 20th century, some professionals adopted names like 'bio park'—a term coined by the National Zoo in Washington D.C. in the late 1980s—or 'conservation park' to distance themselves from 19th-century stereotypes. The New York Zoological Society changed its name to the Wildlife Conservation Society in 1993 and rebranded its zoos as 'wildlife conservation parks'.

The predecessor of the zoological garden is the menagerie. The oldest known zoological collection was discovered in 2009 during excavations at Hierakonpolis, Egypt, dating to approximately 3500 BCE and containing animals such as elephants, baboons, wildcats, hartebeest, and hippos. Other early collections were kept by King Ashur-bel-kala of the Middle Assyrian Empire in the 11th century BC, King Wen of Zhou in China, and various rulers including King Solomon, Queen Semiramis, and King Nebuchadnezzar. By the 4th century BC, zoos existed in most Greek city states; Roman emperors kept private collections for study or use in arenas where animals often fared poorly. In medieval England, King John I created a collection in the Tower of London as early as 1204, which was opened to the public in the 16th century and later moved to the London Zoo. Aztec emperor Moctezuma II also maintained a 'house of animals' in Tenochtitlan, which was destroyed by Hernán Cortés.

The Schönbrunn Zoo in Vienna, Austria, constructed in 1752, is the oldest zoo still in existence. Other early facilities like those in Madrid (1775) and Paris (1795) were founded for scientific research and education. The transition from royal menageries designed to entertain high society into public zoological gardens occurred in the 19th century, with London and Paris serving as models. These modern zoos aimed to educate the general population using modern scientific lines and were often supported by local scientific or commercial societies. In the United Kingdom, the focus was on providing educational exhibits for entertainment and inspiration alongside scientific study.

Animal welfare organizations have criticized zoos and the conditions in which animals are kept. Concerns have been raised regarding visitor behavior and housing conditions and their impact on animal welfare. In recent decades, efforts have been made to improve these conditions.
