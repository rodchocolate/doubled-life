german history

Germany is a federal republic in central-western Europe with a history spanning from prehistoric settlements to its current role as an economic powerhouse within the European Union. Its name derives from 'Germania,' a term used by Julius Caesar for the lands east of the Rhine, where Germanic tribes resisted Roman expansion. The region's political landscape evolved through the Holy Roman Empire, the Protestant Reformation, and 19th-century unification under Prussia.

The concept of Germany as a distinct region traces back to Julius Caesar, who distinguished Germania from Gaul. The Germanic tribes' victory at the Battle of the Teutoburg Forest prevented Roman annexation, though provinces like Germania Superior and Inferior were established along the Rhine. Following the Fall of the Western Roman Empire, the Franks conquered other West Germanic tribes; upon the Frankish Empire's division in 843, the eastern part became East Francia, later the Kingdom of Germany. In 962, Otto I became the first Holy Roman Emperor. During the High Middle Ages, the Hanseatic League dominated trade along the Baltic and North Seas, while the Investiture Controversy saw German Emperors resist Church authority.

In 1517, Martin Luther led the Protestant Reformation, splitting religious allegiance between northern/eastern Protestant states and southern/western Catholic ones. The subsequent Thirty Years' War (1618–48) devastated the Holy Roman Empire, leading to the Peace of Westphalia which granted autonomy to estates like Austria, Prussia, Bavaria, and Saxony. Feudalism fell during the Napoleonic Wars, dissolving the Empire in 1806. Although the German revolutions of 1848–1849 failed, industrialization modernized the economy and elevated Prussia's power. Under Otto von Bismarck, Germany was unified as the German Empire in 1871.

By 1900, Germany had become Europe's dominant industrial power, leading the Central Powers in World War I before its defeat forced it to pay reparations and cede territory. The German Revolution of 1918–1919 established the Weimar Republic. In 1933, Adolf Hitler used economic hardship and resentment over WWI terms to establish a totalitarian Nazi regime that initiated World War II and the Holocaust, which killed six million Jews and eleven million people total. After Germany's defeat in 1945, territories were annexed by Poland and the Soviet Union, and the country was divided during the Cold War into NATO-aligned West Germany and Warsaw Pact-aligned East Germany.

West Germany experienced significant economic expansion, becoming the dominant economy in Western Europe. In 1989, the Berlin Wall was opened following the collapse of the Eastern Bloc, leading to the reunification of East and West Germany in 1990. Franco-German friendship became the foundation for European political integration within the European Union. Germany joined the eurozone as a founding member in 1999 and remains an economic powerhouse, contributing approximately one-quarter of the eurozone's annual gross domestic product.
